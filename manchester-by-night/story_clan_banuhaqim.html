Clan

Banu Haqim

Spinningfields — The Service Alley Behind One of the Towers, 3 A.M.

Adisa moves through the alley the way she moves through everywhere — like the space already belongs to her and she's simply confirming it. She checks the fire escape, the delivery bay shutter, the gap between two bins where someone could crouch and wait. None of it needs checking twice. She does it twice anyway, because the second look is where you catch what the first one missed, and missing things is how people like her stop existing.

A phone buzzes in her jacket. Frida, three messages in a row — a feeding-site inconsistency near the tram stop that probably means nothing, but Frida never lets probably-nothing go until she's turned it over from every angle. Adisa reads them, types back two words, keeps walking. It's the arrangement that works: Frida chases the paperwork and the small stuff no one else has patience for, and Adisa handles whatever the small stuff turns into if it stops being small. Neither of them calls it trust. It functions like trust anyway.

She reaches the end of the alley and stops there, back to the wall, eyes on the street, utterly motionless in a way mortals find vaguely wrong without knowing why. This is the part of the job nobody at court understands — that keeping a perimeter quiet has nothing to do with titles or standing and everything to do with patience, the same patience her clan has always measured in decades rather than nights. Miers thinks he's bought loyalty. He's bought a predator with excellent manners. There's a difference, and she's never once let him forget it.

Something crosses her mind she doesn't often let surface — the last city she did this in, the last perimeter she thought was solid right up until the moment it wasn't. She'd read the signs late there. Not this time. This time she reads everything, the way her line has always read everything, going back to a judgment far older than her own.

A car slows at the far end of the street, headlights sweeping the alley mouth, and Adisa is already still, already watching, already deciding whether it means anything before the driver's even finished the turn — the same cold arithmetic that's kept her alive since long before Manchester.

It doesn't. The car moves on.

She waits another ten seconds anyway, then pushes off the wall and keeps walking her line, unhurried, because she has never once needed to hurry.
