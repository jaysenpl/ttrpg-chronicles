Brujah

Rui's the kind of person a room adjusts around without quite noticing it's doing that. He's been at three separate conversations in the last twenty minutes — a shift worker complaining about her hours, two teenagers arguing about a five-a-side match, an elderly man who just wants someone to actually listen for a change — and each of them would swear, afterward, that Rui had been fully present for theirs and theirs alone.

"You're good at that," says a voice at his elbow. Marcus's usual crowd, but not Marcus himself — one of the regulars, someone who's clocked him here often enough to comment on it.

"At what?"

"Making people feel like the only person in the building."

Rui laughs, easy, unbothered. "It's not a trick. I just like people."

It's true, mostly — right up until the shift worker's voice cracks partway through a sentence about her manager, something about being told she's replaceable, said so casually it barely registers as cruelty to the person who said it. Rui's smile doesn't change. Nothing about him changes, not visibly. But something behind his eyes goes very still for exactly as long as it takes him to decide not to say what he actually wants to say — and whoever's watching closely enough might notice his knuckles have gone white around the coffee cup he isn't drinking from.

It passes. It always passes, out here. He's had a long time to learn where the line is between a Camarilla man's welcome and a Camarilla man's outburst in an Anarch stronghold, and he's never once let the second one slip past the first — not here, not where anyone's watching.

"You alright?" the regular asks.

"Course." The warmth is back before the question's even finished. "Just don't love hearing people talked down to. Old habit."

His phone buzzes. He glances at it, and whatever's left of that stillness gets folded away somewhere else entirely — filed, controlled, gone. He slides the phone back into his pocket.

"Everything alright?"

"Course." He's already turning, already halfway toward someone else's conversation. "Just someone who needs a hand with something. I'll circle back."

He doesn't circle back. Not tonight. Wherever that anger actually goes when nobody's watching, it isn't here — and it isn't small.
