Ventrue

Henry Miers stands at the window with his hands clasped behind his back, the correct posture for a man surveying his domain, even though there's no one here to see him do it. Below, the glass towers hold their light long after the offices have emptied, tended by people whose names he's never had reason to learn — functionaries, several layers down, handling whatever small frictions the night produces so that he never has to. That's rather the arrangement. He decides, from a height no one below him can properly see. What happens after that isn't his concern.

He turns from the window and crosses to the desk, where a single sheet of paper sits squared precisely to its edges — a summary, unsigned, of some minor dispute near the university, already resolved by someone whose title he couldn't name if asked. He initials it without reading past the first line. That's exactly how it should work. A Prince who has to know who handles what isn't much of a Prince at all.

He straightens his cufflinks, though they were already straight, and allows himself, briefly, a thought he'd never let show at court: that this was meant to be further along by now. He'd pictured something closer to what London once had — real precedence, real order, a court that ran on more than his own insistence that it should. Instead there are nights, like this one, when the whole arrangement feels held together by nothing but the fact that no one has yet dared to say otherwise to his face.

He doesn't dwell there long. Dwelling helps nothing.

He thinks instead of the bloodline he carries — Mithras, old beyond old, a name that means something even now, even here, even to Kindred who've never set foot near wherever the truth of it actually sits. It's the one thing no committee, no fractured Camarilla, no fled and frightened Elders can take from him. He didn't earn Manchester. He wasn't given it, either. But the blood is his by right, inherited rather than seized, and on the nights the rest of it feels thin, that's the part he returns to.

He sits, finally, in the chair positioned to face the door — always facing the door — and allows his shoulders to drop half an inch, the only concession he'll make to being alone. Tomorrow the machinery of his court will turn without him needing to lift a finger, and that, too, is exactly how it should work. Tonight, for exactly as long as no one's watching, he lets himself simply be tired.

Then he hears the lift begin its climb, and by the time the doors open, he's sitting up straight again, hands folded, entirely composed.
