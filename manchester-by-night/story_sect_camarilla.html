Camarilla

The glass towers don't sleep, not really — they just change who's awake inside them. By midnight the trading floors have gone dark and the cleaners have gone home, and what's left running the lights is a different kind of business entirely.

Cleo Drummond checks the seating plan for the third time, though she memorised it an hour ago. Merchants' Quay tonight — the private room, the one with the good view of the canal basin and the bad acoustics that somehow nobody ever complains about. She adjusts a place setting by half an inch. It didn't need adjusting.

Below, in the atrium, the ghouled concierge waves through a car that doesn't stop at reception. Above, on the fourteenth floor, a light is on in an office that officially closed for the night four hours ago — the kind of light that isn't for reading by.

Manchester likes to think of itself as a young city on the rise, all glass and ambition and second chances. Cleo has heard that pitch delivered with total sincerity by men who genuinely believe they're building something that will last. She used to find it charming. Lately she just finds it exhausting — all that hope, propped up on so little.

Mark Floros arrives fashionably late with a bottle he insists is for "atmosphere, not drinking," and immediately starts rearranging the flowers she'd already arranged correctly. She lets him. It keeps him busy, and busy is easier to manage than bored.

"Big turnout tonight?" he asks, not really listening for the answer.

"Enough of one," she says.

It's true, in the way most things she says are true — technically, carefully, leaving out exactly as much as needs leaving out. There will be new faces tonight. There are always new faces. Manchester keeps producing them faster than the city can decide what to do with them, and someone has to be ready when they turn up — polished, patient, and paying very close attention to who walks in the door and who they came with.

The elevator chimes. Cleo doesn't look round. She doesn't need to — she's done this enough evenings to have a decent read on how tonight's crowd will size up whoever steps out of it, long before they've said a word.

It's not much of a skill, as skills go. But it's hers, and tonight, in this room, it's enough.
